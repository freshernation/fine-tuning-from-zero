Today's real lesson is not PyTorch. It is this: almost every bug you hit in this course will be a shape bug. A model is a pile of numbers arranged in boxes, and training is arithmetic between boxes. If you can say the shape of every box out loud, you can debug anything in the next six weeks.

Read a shape from the outside in. (2, 3) is two things, each of which is three numbers. (4, 8, 16) is four things, each of which is eight things, each of which is sixteen numbers.

: means "all of this dimension". A plain number removes that dimension. That is why x[0] has shape (3,) and not (1, 3).

dim=1 does not mean "sum the columns". It means "dimension 1 is the one that gets squashed". Before you run a line with dim= in it, cross that number out of the shape and say what is left.

When two tensors have different shapes, PyTorch lines the shapes up from the right and stretches any dimension of size 1 to fit.

No error. Wrong answer. Lined up from the right, (2,) matched the columns, so each column got a discount instead of each row. Broadcasting never complains when the sizes happen to fit. A shape bug that crashes is a gift; the ones that run are the ones that cost you an evening.

Training a model is one idea repeated a few million times: measure how wrong you are, find which way is less wrong, move a little that way. Today you do it once, on one number, with a pencil. Everything after today is the same thing with more numbers.

Suppose x = 3, the right answer is y = 10, and the weight is currently w = 2. The prediction is 6. It is wrong by 4.

We want one number that is big when the model is badly wrong and zero when it is right. Squaring the error does that:

The data (x, y) is fixed. The only thing you are allowed to change is w. So the loss is really a function of w: try a different w, get a different loss.

Nudge w up a hair, from 2 to 2.001. The loss goes from 16 to about 15.976. It went down by 0.024 for a nudge of 0.001 — a slope of about −24.

That slope is the gradient. Its sign tells you the direction (negative: increasing w reduces the loss) and its size tells you how steep.

You do not have to nudge. Calculus gives the slope exactly, and you already have what you need: the loss is something squared, and the something is wx - y. The chain rule says: the slope of the outside, times the slope of the inside.

Derive it on paper now. Check it against the nudge: at w = 2, x = 3, y = 10 your formula must give −24. That formula is exercise 1.

lr is the learning rate: how big "a little" is. Too small and you crawl. Too big and you jump clean over the valley and land higher up the other side.

The task is "predict what comes next". So for every position, the right answer is simply the character after it:

Each row of a batch is a stretch like this cut from a random place. blocksize is how long the stretch is; batchsize is how many you cut. So x and y are both (B, T) whole numbers.

For each position the model outputs one score per character in the vocabulary. Those scores are called logits. They are not probabilities: they can be negative, and they do not add up to one. Softmax turns them into probabilities:

Shapes: (B, T) goes in, (B, T, C) comes out, where C is the vocabulary size. Monday's letters, all three at once.

For each position, look at the probability the model gave to the character that actually came next. Cross-entropy is the negative log of that probability, averaged over every position.

A model that knows nothing spreads its bets evenly: probability 1/C on everything. Its loss is -ln(1/C), which is ln(C). With 65 characters that is about 4.17.

Remember that number. A fresh model should start near ln(vocabsize) — usually a little above it, never far. If yours starts at 9, it is not "bad", it is broken. That check costs nothing and you should make it every time you build a model.

The score between position i and position j is the dot product of i's query with j's key: multiply them number by number and add up. If they point the same way the score is large; if they are unrelated it is near zero.

Do that for every pair and you have a (T, T) table of scores. Row i is "how interested is position i in each other position". That is the weight matrix, almost.

Each of the three is made by a plain linear layer, with no bias, from the position's vector. Nobody tells those layers what to look for. Training does.

Scores can be any size and any sign. Weights have to be positive and add up to one. You know the function that does that: softmax, along each row.

Here is the observation the paper rests on. When you fine-tune a big model on a narrow task, the change ΔW turns out to be simple. Not small in size — simple in structure. It can be written, nearly, as the product of two thin matrices:

That is LoRA. Low-Rank Adaptation: adapt the model by learning a low-rank change to its weights.

A squeezes the input from din numbers down to r. B expands those r back out to dout. So whatever the change is, it has to pass through a bottleneck r numbers wide. A full ΔW could do anything to x; yours can only do things that survive that squeeze.

The bet is that fine-tuning for one task does not need more. It is a bet, and on Friday you will measure it: you will train at ranks 1, 4 and 16 and see what each buys.

Your first exercise builds B @ A in full and asks PyTorch for its rank. It will never be more than r, however large the matrix. That is the meaning of the word.

Work one more yourself, with your own numbers, both ways, before you write any code.

B starts as all zeros. Then B @ A is zero, the right-hand road contributes nothing, and on step 0 the layer behaves exactly as the pretrained layer did. You begin from the model as it was — not from the model plus some random disturbance.

A starts random. Small random numbers, the same way PyTorch initialises an ordinary Linear: nn.init.kaiminguniform(A, a=math.sqrt(5)).

Why not both at zero? One of today's tests checks which of A and B receives a gradient on the very first backward pass. Run it, look at what it asserts, and work out the answer to this question from that. You will be asked on Friday.

alpha is a number you choose alongside the rank. It sets how loudly the adapter speaks relative to the frozen layer. Dividing by r means that if you double the rank you do not also have to retune everything else: the overall size of the update stays roughly where it was.

In practice people set alpha to once or twice the rank and leave it. It is a knob you need to know exists, and to put in the right place.

You can keep many adapters for one base model — one per task — and each costs megabytes. An adapter is useless without the exact base model it was trained on. It is a change; a change to something else is noise. An adapter only fits a model with adapters in the same places, at the same rank.
